## Beta Delphini A

The brightest star in the constellation, β Delphini A, has an interesting back
story.

In 1814, Giuseppe Piazzi published the
[Praecipuarum Stellarum Inerrantium Positiones Mediae](https://books.google.com/books?id=Go5xQ69sfsAC&pg=PA147),
a catalogue of star positions, with the help of Niccolò Cacciatore. On page 147
(in Hora XX) on rows 227 and 254, you can find (for the first time) stars named
ROTANEV and SVALOCIN.

It wasn't until 1859 when Thomas William Webb published the
[Celestial Objects for Common Telescopes](https://books.google.com/books?id=5DoDAAAAQAAJ&pg=PA193)
that the origin of these names was uncovered. Starting on page 193:

> The leaders of this little, compact, fish-like constellation, α and β, are
> distinguished by names which, even among the multifarious disfigurements of
> Oriental words so abundant in the heavens, are pre-eminently strange, Svalocin
> and Rotanev. The former Smyth has justly characterized as "cacophonous and
> barbaric," and says that "no poring into the black letter versions of the
> Almagest, El Báttaní, Ibn Yúnis, and other authorities, enables one to form
> any rational conjecture as to the mis-reading, mis-writing, or
> mis-application, in which so strange a metamorphosis could have originated."
> And of _Rotanev_ he observes, "the which putteth derivation and etymology at
> defiance." Where so eminent and accomplished a scholar and antiquarian has not
> succeeded, it would seem presumptuous to offer a solution, but that accident
> is sometimes more fortunate than study; and if the following is not after all
> the right key, it certainly is a marvel that is should open the lock so
> readily. The letters of these strange words, reversed, form
> <span class="small-caps">Nicolaus Venator</span>, in which it is easy to
> recognize a Latin version of the name of <span class="small-caps">Niccolo
> Cacciatore</span>, assistant at the Palermo Observatory, in the Catalogue
> emanating from which these stars are so denominated.

# Why I love this

For so, so many reasons.

First, Cacciatore decided to translate his last name (meaning hunter in Italian)
to Latin, getting Venator. Then flipped it backwards to get Rotanev, secretly
naming the celestial body after himself.

Next, nobody knew why it had this name for 45 years (!), until Webb comes along
and figured it out. This dude would have absolutely loved
<a class="link" data-x="1" data-y="-2">puzzle hunts</a> and his write up was
both savage and brilliant.
